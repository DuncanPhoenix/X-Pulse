Motivation




Modern 4th-generation X-ray facilities provide unprecedented opportunities for probing ultrafast dynamics. Their exceptionally high photon flux per pulse (109 - 1012 photons per pulse), combined with ultra-short pulse duration (~10 fs and ~100 ps at XFEL’s and synchrotrons) with pulse megahertz repetition rates, enables ultra-fast single-shot/single exposure imaging of stochastic phenomena in matter.

HISTORY

The history of speed photography evolved from capturing mechanical events visible to the human eye to using highly specialized lasers to probe subatomic-level ultrafast processes. Breakthroughs were driven by innovations in photographic technology and lighting, revealing hidden aspects of motion for both scientific analysis and artistic expression.

Pioneers and chronophotography: Eadweard Muybridge (1870s) pioneered the photographic study of motion using a battery of cameras triggered by tripwires. His 1878 series, The Horse in Motion, famously proved that a horse lifts all four hooves off the ground at the same time during a gallop. This work was foundational for the development of cinematography.

Étienne-Jules Marey (1880s) developed the field of chronophotography by using a single camera to record successive images of motion. He invented a photographic gun to take 12 pictures per second and later a continuous-film chronophotograph that was a precursor to the modern film camera. Marey’s work often presented multiple exposures on a single plate to visually trace the path of movement.

Georges Demeny and Ottomar Anschutz refined chronophotography by creating the stroboscope for projecting images of motion. Independently, Anschutz developed cameras with focal-plane shutters for exposures as fast as 1/1000th of a second and invented a viewer that used electric sparks to illuminate chronophotographs.

Early flash photography (1850s-1920s) evolved from dangerous explosive flash powder to more practical systems. Harold “Doc” Edgerton (1930s) pioneered the use of the electronic stroboscope to “stop” fast motion and produce crisp, instantaneous photos, including iconic images such as a splashing milk drop and a bullet piercing an apple. His work had both scientific and artistic applications.

Ultrafast events and modern methods

Rapatronic camera (1950s): During the Cold War, Edgerton and his company developed the rapatronic camera to photograph nuclear tests. It used a magneto-optical shutter with no moving parts to capture exposures as short as 10 nanoseconds.

Electronic imaging sensors (1980s-1990s): The invention and refinement of CCD and CMOS sensors revolutionized high-speed photography. Later CMOS sensors offered high flexibility by allowing a tradeoff between frame rate and resolution.

Pump-probe spectroscopy (1980s): Ahmed Zewail pioneered the pump-probe method using ultrafast lasers to study chemical reactions at the femtosecond scale (10-15 seconds). This technique, known as femtochemistry, earned him the Nobel Prize in 1999.

Compressed Ultrafast Photography (CUP) (2014): Researchers developed CUP as a single-shot imaging technique capable of capturing non-repetitive events at an astonishing 1011 frames per second.
